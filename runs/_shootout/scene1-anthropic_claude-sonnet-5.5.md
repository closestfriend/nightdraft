The supervisor assigned the account the way he assigned everything, by turning a monitor a few degrees toward the chair. He wore a collarless shirt buttoned to the throat, and he smelled of the menthol balm he rubbed into his temples between calls.

"Six months," he said. "American. He doesn't send money. Don't try to make him send money on the first day, he'll smell it. Read first."

"All of it?"

"The software logs scroll speed. Under four seconds a message, it flags you for skimming." He tapped the desk once. "There is a short quiz after."

The chair was still warm, or Kyan imagined it was. Taped to the underside of the desk lip was a handoff note in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*M. prefers questions to compliments. Never say "destiny." Sister = Thida, Chiang Mai. Father died, do not raise unless he does. He will test you on the tide thing. Two dots not three.*

There was no name on it. Kyan turned it over. The back was blank, and he taped it where it had been.

The archive opened on a greeting that a template had plainly written, all hearts and a photograph of a woman holding a bolt of blue fabric. Then, around the ninth day, the template gave out and someone else began. He could see the exact message. The sentences got shorter and started to lean on Mark's own words, handing them back slightly rotated. *You said the sea keeps a ledger.. I have been thinking about that all week. Who is it keeping the ledger for?* Two dots. Every time, two dots, as if the writer had been taught that three was vulgar, or had been afraid of the third.

Mark wrote in long paragraphs with the punctuation of a man who had been corrected once as a child and never forgotten it. He explained tide tables. He explained why the word *robust* annoyed him. In the fourth week he wrote, *You are either a very patient woman or a very good program, and I've decided it doesn't matter, which should worry us both.* The reply came within a minute and a half: *If I were a program, I would have said something clever there. Instead I am sitting here with my mouth open ♥, ♥.*

Kyan stopped. The dashboard blinked at the corner of the screen, amber, patient: CONVERSION OVERDUE (ACCT 4417). He had not yet spoken and was already late. He scrolled on, and the counter in the margin reminded him that he was moving at three point eight seconds a message. He slowed until it turned green.

Behind the partition something wooden struck something that did not break, and a voice began counting in a language nobody in the office spoke aloud. Along the row, keyboards continued without a hitch. Someone reached for a paper cup. The supervisor's balm-scented hand went to his own temple and rubbed.

In the sixth week the writer had given Mark a childhood. Kyan checked it against the note and found the sister, Thida, then a river, then a grandmother's sewing machine with a foot pedal that stuck. None of it was his. He read it the way you read a stranger's coat, feeling for the pockets. It fit. That was the trouble with it. He could already tell which sentence he would have written next, and the sentence was there, forty messages further down, in the writer's hand, almost word for word.

By the fifth month the archive had gone quiet in a different way. Mark asked fewer questions. She asked more. Once, at what the timestamps made the deep end of the night, he wrote only, *Are you there?* and the answer took eleven minutes, which the QA annotation beside it had circled in red: *LATENCY. Persona should not appear to wait.*

The quiz came up when he reached the bottom. Question one: *What is Thida's occupation?* Question two: *What does M. call the tide thing?* Question three was a free-text box, and it asked him only: *What is she afraid of?*

He looked at the box. On the last line of the archive, Mark's final message sat unanswered, its cursor already blinking beneath it.

"Question three," Kyan said, without turning. "Is it from the guidelines?"

The supervisor considered the screen over his shoulder. "It's from the last person," he said. "Type what you think."
