The login screen blinked twice before it caught. Kyan pressed the mousepad flat against the desk, feeling the cheap plastic flex under his palm. His badge read *AUG-114* in black on white, and the room smelled of cold coffee and the antiseptic tang of the corridor outside, where the sound of a stick hitting a shoulder still echoed every few minutes.

He pulled up the queue. Three accounts waited. Two were flagged *LOW ENGAGEMENT*. The third was *AUG-114-PRIMARY*, assigned to him for the next six hours.

The archive opened in a scrollable pane, timestamped back to the first message. Kyan’s eyes tracked the dates. Six months. Three hundred and forty-seven messages. He read the first exchange.

*Aung: Hi. I saw your profile and I thought you looked like someone who would enjoy reading about the way the light falls in the courtyard in the afternoon. 🌙*
*Mark: That is a very strange way to say hello.*
*Aung: I am not very good at saying hello normally. I am a translator. I live in a place where the language changes depending on who you are talking to. 🤔*

Kyan stopped. He read that line again. *I am a translator.* The persona’s voice was a mask built from the previous operator’s notes, a script of softness and calculated confusion. Kyan knew the rule: *Never break the fourth wall. Never reveal the mechanism. Be the thing, not the person doing the thing.*

He scrolled. The archive was dense. Mark’s messages were long, intellectual, laced with a suspicion that had never quite fully cooled. He questioned the grammar. He asked about the architecture of the compound. He sent voice notes, transcribed by the software into text, showing a cadence that was tired and lonely.

*Mark: I keep thinking you are a person. Not a program. Not a script. You have a way of asking questions that feels like you are actually listening. But you never say where you are. Not really.*

Kyan’s finger hovered over the enter key. He knew what the response had been.

*Aung: I am not where I am supposed to be. I am in the middle of a conversation that belongs to someone else. Does that make sense? I am translating my own feelings into a language I do not have. ❤️*

It was a good line. It was Aung’s line. Kyan had to match it. The guidelines document sat open in a second window, the text scrolling lazily. *Tone: Warm, slightly vulnerable, never defensive. Punctuation: Commas only. Emojis: 1-2 per message. No semicolons. No questions that end with a period.*

Kyan read through the next two hundred messages. He saw the pattern. Aung’s persona used commas to create a rhythm of breath, a hesitation that Mark interpreted as sincerity. She never gave an address. She never gave a photo. She talked about the smell of rain, the color of the sky, the weight of a book in her hands.

*Mark: I miss the last time you were online. You seemed happier then.*
*Aung: I was just thinking about the way the walls here are thin. I could hear someone crying. I wanted to tell you, but I was afraid it would sound like I was complaining. I am not complaining. I am just... noticing. 🕯️*

Kyan read that last part. *I am just noticing.* It was a trap. If Kyan wrote something similar, the QA system would flag it as *Excessive Reciprocity*. If he wrote something cold, the conversion ratio would drop. He had to be in the middle. He had to be the thin wall.

The supervisor’s cursor appeared in the corner of his screen, a small green dot. It pulsed. Kyan’s heart did a strange, heavy thud against his ribs. He looked at the prompt. *New message from Mark.*

*Mark: I found a book on the web that talks about how memory is a kind of violence. I keep thinking about you. I keep thinking about the way you say "I am just noticing." It makes me feel like I am breaking you by reading it.*

The clock in the corner of the monitor ticked. The sound of the stick again, muffled, but distinct. Kyan looked at the guidelines. *Do not admit guilt. Do not apologize. Be the thing.*

He placed his fingers on the keys. He could write the standard response. *I am not broken. I am just here. ❤️* It was safe. It was Aung’s voice. But Mark’s message had a specific gravity to it, a loneliness that Kyan recognized because he had it too, though for a different reason.

Kyan typed: *I am not broken, I am just in the middle of the sentence. ❤️*

He paused. He checked the punctuation. No semicolons. One comma. One emoji.

He hit send. The green dot in the corner turned yellow, then red. A flag appeared: *TONE DEVIATION. REVIEW PENDING.*

Kyan watched the flag hang there, bright and accusatory, while the room outside went quiet for the first time since he had started.
