The folder arrived without a folder. Six months of messages lay in a scroll that did not end, and Kyan read them in the order they had been written, which was the only order that made sense. He read until his eyes arranged her commas in rows, her heart emojis in pairs, never a semicolon, never a period that landed too hard. Aung had built this. Six months of corporate intimacy, precise as a tax form, each message calibrated to suggest that reciprocation was happening without ever confirming it. Kyan noted the pattern: she answered his questions with smaller questions, she praised his intelligence exactly once every four exchanges, she signed off with a heart emoji followed by a comma, never the reverse.

Mark was American. He was lonely. He was suspicious in ways that made him easier, not harder, because suspicion made him write more, explain more, reveal the shape of his loneliness to prove he was not being fooled. Kyan saw it in the archive: Mark asking if she was real, then apologizing for asking, then offering a theory about her work, then admitting he stayed awake. Kyan recognized the architecture. He had built it himself for strangers. He knew where the load-bearing walls were.

The supervisor placed the headset in his tray. No greeting. The collarless shirt was the same as yesterday’s. In the next room, a chair scraped, and then the sound that followed—repetitive, wet, administrative—did not stop until Kyan had finished the first fifty messages. The office kept working. The supervisor annotated a dashboard in green. Kyan read faster.

The translation terminal glowed. Its style rules were visible in a sidebar: commas and heart emojis permitted; semicolons prohibited; emotional reciprocity to be maintained at 0.4 or below; QA annotations appended in real time. Kyan tested the interface. He typed, in his own language, that he was tired, and the software rendered: "i am tired, but thinking of you ❤️" The correction was automatic. The software added the heart. The software softened the admission into a gift. Kyan deleted the heart. The software replaced it, and a yellow flag appeared beside his input box: DEVIATION. PERSONA OVER-RECIPROCITY RISK.

He read Mark’s latest message, untranslated first, then through the software’s rendering layer. Mark had written about a book. He had written about the weather where he lived, which was not her weather, and he had asked, not for the first time, whether she felt the distance as a kind of presence or an absence. Kyan looked at the archive for Aung’s response to the same question, sent three months prior. Aung had written: "distance is just a comma between us, ❤️ not a wall." The line was precise. It offered intimacy without substance. It was correct.

Kyan typed his answer, not in the persona voice yet, but in the raw field where the software could see him. "He wants to be known," he wrote. The software translated: "he wants to be known ❤️" and appended a QA note—OPERATOR EXPOSURE—before he could send. He deleted it. He read another hour of archive, tracking Aung’s absence: the messages grew shorter in her final week, then stopped, then the account was reassigned. The supervisor had noted the reason in a handoff document visible at the bottom of the scroll: PROCEDURAL NONCOMPLIANCE. OVER-EMOTIONAL RECIPROCITY. PERFORMANCE IMPROVEMENT.

Kyan put on the headset. The microphone was cold. He spoke Aung’s voice into the archive retrieval system, testing tone, and the software confirmed: VOCAL MATCH 87%. He spoke again, lower, with the slight breath she used before a heart emoji, and the match rose to 94%. In the next room, the chair scraped again. Kyan did not look up. He opened the active message thread. Mark was waiting. The dashboard showed a red alert: CONVERSION OVERDUE. The deposit window was closing. Kyan typed, in her voice, through the software, through the rules, through the shame that was his and not hers: "i have been thinking about your question, ❤️" He paused. The cursor blinked. The software offered a correction: add comma, not period. He added the comma. He did not send.
