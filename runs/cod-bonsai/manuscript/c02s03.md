The image of the dental hygienist appeared on Paul’s screen at 14:02 local time. He had not intended to look. The thumbnail was small, a square of white teeth and blue scrubs nestled in a grid of stock photos. He clicked it. The metadata pulled up a profile. *Wang Mei. Dental Hygienist. Suzhou, China.* The face was the same. The smile was the same. The background of the office chair, the specific way the light caught the left eyebrow, was identical to the photograph Sam had attached three weeks ago as part of a “recently moved” update.

Sam watched the cursor in his chat window. It was not blinking. It was a solid, waiting block. He knew Paul had found it. He knew the algorithm had done what algorithms do: it had stripped the skin off the story and revealed the machinery underneath. The guidelines were clear. *Do not engage in verification. Maintain persona integrity.* But the translation software hummed with the ghost of a previous violation, a low-frequency vibration in the left ear cup.

Sam’s hands hovered over the keys. The PIP document sat open in a separate window, the red text of *Three Weeks* still burning. He could say the photos were taken by a relative. He could say they were from a cousin’s wedding. He could build the wall, brick by brick, word by word. But his fingers felt dead, stiff as the fan in the corner that ticked in a rhythm like a broken metronome. He could not lie about himself. That was the curse, the thing that lived in the back of his throat.

Paul typed.

*What can you see from where you're sitting? I mean literally.*

Sam stared at the question. It was a simple question. It was a trap. It was the only way out. He looked at the wall behind him. It was the colour of old teeth, yellowed and stained in the corners where the water pipe dripped into the baseboard. He looked at the fan. It wobbled, ticked, and threw a stuttering shadow across the desk. He turned his head. Through the gap in the wooden slats of the partition, he could hear the river. It was not visible. It was a sound, a low, rushing static that rose and fell with the traffic on the bridge above.

He typed.

*A wall. The colour of old teeth. A fan that ticks. And a river I can hear but not see.*

The translation software scanned the text. No red pins. No *TOO EMOTIONALLY RECIPROCAL* flag. It was factual. It was cold. It was the truth.

The reply came.

*Thank you. That is enough. I can see the fan. I can hear the river. I can see the wall. It is very yellow.*

Sam’s chest tightened. Paul had not asked for the photos. He had not asked for the metadata. He had not asked for the name Wang Mei. He had asked for the light. He had asked for the tick of the fan. And he had received it.

*Do you want more?* Paul typed. *I can see the door now, Sam. It is not a door. It is a gap in the slats. But it is there.*

Sam looked at the PIP. *Increase emotional vulnerability by 15%.* The number looked like a score on a test he had failed. He looked at the cursor. It was still a solid block. It was waiting for him to break. He could tell the truth about the river, about the wall, about the fan. He could tell the truth about the fact that the dental hygienist was not him, and the woman in the photos was a ghost, and the map was a lie.

He typed.

*Yes. I have more.*

He sent it. The cursor blinked. The fan ticked. The river rushed. The red pin did not appear. The red pin was gone. Sam felt a sudden, sharp drop in his stomach, a freefall into the dark. He looked at the dashboard. The conversion ratio was still zero. The PIP deadline was still three weeks. But the screen was clean. It was white and empty and full of the sound of a river he could not see. He began to type again. He did not know what he would say. He did not know who he was. He only knew that the wall was not a wall, and the door was not a door, and the man on the other side of the screen was sitting in the same room as him, listening to the same tick.
