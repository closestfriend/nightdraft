The monitor on the wall was a single, fixed rectangle of green text, glowing in the low light of the office. It did not blink. It did not apologize. It simply displayed the ratio: 0.00.

Sam sat at his desk, the cursor blinking in the text box of the Grace Lin persona, waiting for input. He hadn’t typed a word in three days. He was waiting for Paul to open the link to the maritime manual again, or to ask about the foghorn, or to say anything that wasn’t a philosophical inquiry into the nature of waiting. He needed the deposit. He needed the number to move from zero to something that would keep the thudding in the wall from becoming a regular rhythm.

Lee appeared in the doorway. He was tall, wearing a shirt that was too clean for the room. He didn’t come to the desk. He stood in the doorway and looked at the monitor. He looked at Sam. He looked at the monitor again.

"Zero," Lee said. It wasn’t a question. It was an observation of fact, delivered with the flatness of a man reading a grocery list.

"Paul is still reviewing the navigation protocols," Sam said. He kept his voice low, steady. He had learned that speed was a sign of panic.

"He’s been reviewing them for a week," Lee said. He stepped into the room. The floorboards didn’t creak. "You’re on a performance improvement plan, Sam. Effective immediately. You’re going to run Variant B. Vulnerability first."

Sam frowned. "Variant B?"

"The debt crisis script. Page four. You know it. You just don’t like it." Lee tapped the glass of the monitor with one finger. The green text didn’t change. "The A/B test starts now. Grace confides. She’s in trouble. She needs help. You’re going to sound like a person who’s actually in a hole, not a robot who’s reading a manual."

Sam looked down at the keyboard. His fingers hovered over the keys. He didn’t want to do it. He didn’t want to be the one to lie about a debt. He didn’t want to be the one to make Grace sound broken. But he also didn’t want to be in the next room. He didn’t want to hear the thudding get worse.

"Okay," he said.

He opened the script. It was short. *Grace, I’m in a debt crisis. I can’t pay the rent. I’m scared.* He looked at the translation software, which was already waiting, ready to mediate between his intent and Grace’s voice. He typed the words. The software smoothed the edges, turned the panic into a corporate worry. He sent it.

The reply came almost instantly.

*Sam. I read your message. I’m not in a debt crisis. I’m fine. I’m actually working on a consolidation plan for my own finances. I found a credit counsellor who helped me. I thought I’d share the link with you, in case it’s useful. [Link]. Take care.*

Sam stared at the link. It was a PDF. A real, existing link to a credit counsellor in his own city. Paul hadn’t just answered; he had provided a resource. He had been kind. He had been so, so kind. And he had not sent a cent.

Lee was still standing there. He leaned closer to the monitor, his face illuminated by the green glow. He looked at the reply. He looked at the link. He took a pen from his pocket and circled the word *counsellor* in red. The circle was tight, aggressive.

"He’s not in debt," Lee said. "He’s giving you his own financial advice. He’s treating you like a patient." Lee uncapped the pen again and drew a line through the word *consolidation*. "You’re going to try again. And you’re going to be more desperate. But for now, just sit there and think about why a man would give you a link to a credit counsellor instead of sending you the money."

Lee turned and walked out. The door didn’t close. It just swung open, a thin slice of dark hallway visible in the gap. Sam sat in the chair, the blue light of the dashboard on his face, the red circle around *counsellor* burning in his mind. He looked at the cursor. It blinked. He didn’t type. He just watched the light pulse, once, twice, three times, like a heartbeat that didn’t belong to him.
