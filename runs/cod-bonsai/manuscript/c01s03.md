The cursor blinked in the text box, a steady, impatient pulse.

"Paul Whitaker," Sam thought, the name sitting on the tongue like a stone. He leaned back, the chair groaning. The dashboard glowed blue. *CONVERSION OVERDUE: 14 DAYS.* The number stared at him, red and flat. He needed a deposit. He needed a sentence.

Paul had just sent a file. It was a PDF, dense with text. *Harbour Pilot Manual, Chapter 4: Tides and Fog.* A footnote on page 32 read: *When the pilot’s voice is lost to the mist, the vessel must trust the water, for the water does not lie.*

Sam scrolled the archive. Ana had written to Paul about this. *The water is a liar,* she had said. *It moves when it is not supposed to.* Sam had deleted that message. Grace did not lie about the water. Grace maintained protocols.

Sam’s fingers hovered over the keys. The software was watching. A red pin hovered in the corner of the screen, waiting for punctuation, waiting for a word that wasn’t allowed. He had to answer. The silence was getting too thick. If he didn’t type, the thudding in the wall would stop, and he didn’t know what would come out of that dark.

He began to type.

"Regarding the footnote on page 32," he wrote. "The phrase 'trust the water' is a common idiom. It suggests the water is reliable. However, water is fluid. It changes shape. It does not trust; it flows. If the pilot’s voice is lost, the vessel must trust its own instruments. The water is not the pilot. The water is the road. The road does not know the driver."

The red pin dropped. *GRAMMAR ERROR DETECTED: UNEXPECTED PUNCTUATION.*

Sam froze. He looked at the sentence again. He had typed a semicolon. *The water is not the pilot; it is the road.* He hadn’t noticed it. He hadn’t meant it. But it was there, a small, sharp cut in the text.

He looked at the dashboard. The red pin was still there. A second pin appeared. *STYLE VIOLATION: POETIC METAPHOR DETECTED.*

He didn’t delete it. He couldn’t. The sentence was already out of his hands. It was in the queue, waiting to be sent. The software was processing it. *Sending…*

The message sent.

Sam leaned forward, watching the screen. The blue light of the dashboard reflected in his eyes. He had broken the rule. He had written a metaphor. He had used a semicolon. He had spoken in a voice that was not Grace’s voice. He had spoken in his own.

The reply came in.

Not the usual long, philosophical essay. Not the transactional questions about verification codes. This was short. It was immediate.

*Who are you tonight?*

Sam stared at the words. They were not a question about the manual. They were not a request for a deposit. They were a question about him. About the voice that had just left the screen.

The red pin on the dashboard pulsed. *VIOLATION LOGGED: Semicolon. Metaphor. Unapproved tone.*

Lee’s name was on the log. *First violation. Operator: Sam. Account: Grace Lin. Time: 02:14.*

Sam did not know what to do. The message was already out. Paul had read it. Paul had seen the seam.

He looked at the screen. The cursor blinked. He could delete the message. He could apologize. He could say, *I am Grace. I am a protocol. I am a template.* But he couldn’t delete the semicolon. He couldn’t delete the memory of his mother saving the heels of bread for the birds. He couldn’t delete the foghorn that he had typed without noticing, a small, sharp sound in the dark.

He sat there, the blue light on his face, the thudding in the wall growing louder.
