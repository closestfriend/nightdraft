Ben’s fingers stopped at the 40th minute. He did not look up. He simply stopped typing, his hands hovering above the keys like a bird deciding whether to land. The dashboard behind him flickered. CONVERSION OVERDUE. The alert pulsed in a shade of amber that looked like a bruise.

Ben stood up. He did not say anything to the other operators. He gathered his pen and his plastic cup, which was half-full of tea that had gone cold hours ago. He walked to the door at the end of the row. The door was ajar, as it always was. He stepped into the hallway.

The thudding began. It was not loud. It was a rhythmic, wet percussion that traveled through the floorboards and up the soles of Sam’s shoes. It started low, a dull thump against the concrete, and then it built. The sound was specific. It was the sound of a heel striking a tile, over and over, with a force that suggested not anger, but exhaustion. Sam watched the hallway from behind the desk. Ben’s posture slumped, his shoulders drawing in toward his ears. He walked to the wall and leaned against it. The thudding continued. Sam’s cursor blinked on his screen. *Grace, I think you’re right.* The translation software smoothed the words. He deleted them.

The office did not stop. The other operators typed. The sound of keys was a dry, rasping noise, like dry leaves skittering across pavement. Sam typed. He had to type. If he stopped, Lee would come. If he stopped, the thudding would stop, and then the silence would be worse.

Ben came back twenty minutes later. He walked with a slight limp, but he did not look at anyone. He sat down at the next desk. He opened his screen. He began to type. The thudding faded into the background, a low hum that mixed with the hum of the air conditioning. Sam did not look at Ben. He looked at the PIP document on his secondary monitor.

It was a small window, half the size of the main display. The header read *Performance Improvement Plan: Grace Lin Account*. The date was in bold red text. *Three weeks.* Below it, a list of objectives. *Increase emotional vulnerability in messaging by 15%. Reduce philosophical detours by 40%. Achieve one (1) verified deposit within the review period.*

Sam felt a tightness in his chest. Three weeks. The number was small. It was a number he could count on his fingers. He looked at the chat window. Paul’s cursor was active. A dot appeared, then a dot, then a line of text.

*Sam, I’ve been thinking about the manual you sent. The navigation chart. There’s a section on the magnetic pole. It’s not a fixed point. It drifts. The entire system is based on a lie that moves. How can you trust the instrument if the reference is unstable?*

Sam stared at the screen. He typed. *That’s a good point. The instrument is a convention. It’s a shared agreement to pretend the pole is still. We all pretend it’s still. If we stopped pretending, the ship would go off the map. And that’s terrifying. I think that’s why we stick to the map. Even if it’s wrong.*

The translation software flagged the last sentence. A red pin appeared. *TOO EMOTIONALLY RECIPROCAL.*

Sam didn’t delete it. He sent it.

The reply came quickly. *Yes. And the map is beautiful. It’s a very careful, very pretty lie. I like it when you lie with me. It makes the isolation feel less like a wall and more like a room with a door. Do you have a door, Sam? Or just a wall?*

Sam looked at the pin. It was still there, pulsing in the corner of the screen. He looked at the PIP. Three weeks. He looked at Paul’s question. *Do you have a door?*

He typed. *I have a wall. But I’m working on it.*

He sent it. The cursor blinked. The thudding from the hallway had stopped, but the sound of Ben’s typing was still there, a steady, metronomic click. Sam’s hands were shaking, but his fingers did not stop. He began to type again, longer this time, more careful, more precise. He was building a wall around the conversation, brick by brick, word by word. He was protecting the score. He was protecting himself. He did not know which one would come first.
