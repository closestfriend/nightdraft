The guidelines document for Grace Lin sat in a window that blocked the rest of the screen. *Style: Professional, warm but restrained.* The margins were tight. The font was Arial, size ten.

Sam read the syntax rules first. They were not suggestions. They were the architecture. *Use commas and hearts. Never semicolons.*

He highlighted the examples. The correct way to close a thought was a small, bright thing. A heart. A comma. No periods that felt like slamming a door. The software flagged violations in real time, a little red pin dropping into the text box like a nail in a floorboard. *Grammar error detected: Semicolon usage violates persona style.*

He looked at the archive. It was not just a log. It was a body of water.

Sam scrolled back to the beginning. The first operator had been named Ana. The metadata did not say Ana, of course. It said *User: A_882*. But the voice was a fingerprint.

Sam found her tells.

She opened a thought with *hm,* followed by a space. Not a capital H. Just a hush. It was a soft landing, a breath before the sentence. Sam typed it out to feel the shape of it. *hm,* it felt like a hand on a shoulder.

She spelled *grey* the British way. No *gray*. No American hesitation. It was a small, precise act of geography. *The sky was grey.* *The rain was grey.* *The light in the kitchen was grey.*

And the sign-offs. This was the part that made Sam’s stomach drop. She ended long, complex letters with a lowercase *ok?*

It was not a question. It was a surrender. A small, lowercase surrender that said, *I have given you everything I have, and now I am waiting to be told if it is enough.*

Sam catalogued these. He thought of them as Ana’s. He did not know if Ana was a person or a ghost or a role played by three different bodies in the same chair. He only knew the hand.

He scrolled to month three.

This was the masterpiece.

The subject line was blank. The content was a description of a ferry crossing. Not the logistics of the fare, not the schedule, but the way the water moved against the hull. *The metal groaned, a low, tectonic sound. The smell of diesel and salt hung in the air, thick and indifferent.*

Sam read it again. The ferry was a metaphor for the account. The crossing was the time between deposit and trust.

Then, the kitchen. A grandmother’s kitchen.

Ana described a kitchen that was not hers. The tiles were chipped. The radio played a song that was too loud. The grandmother was not present, only the heat from the stove. *The steam from the kettle rose in a straight line, then broke, scattering into the air.*

Sam read Paul’s reply to that letter. It was not a transaction. It was not a question about the verification code.

*I have printed it,* Paul had written. *I pinned it above my desk. I look at it when I cannot remember if I am alone or if I am just being ignored by the people around me. It is the first time someone has described the sound of a boat without apologizing for it.*

Sam stared at the screen. *A_882* had not converted Paul in month three. She had not asked for the deposit. She had not checked the identity. She had simply handed him a kitchen and a boat and said, *here, look at this.*

And Paul had looked.

Sam scrolled forward.

Month four.

The hand was gone.

The letters were still warm, still professional, still using the British *grey*. But the *hm,* was gone. The lowercase *ok?* was replaced by a standard, corporate sign-off: *Best regards, Grace Lin.*

The ferry had been replaced by a mention of "account security protocols." The grandmother’s kitchen was gone, replaced by a discussion of "digital asset verification."

The voice was still there, but the person who had written it had been removed. The account had been handed over. The persona had been maintained, but the soul had been scrubbed.

Sam looked at the cursor. It blinked in the text box, a steady, impatient pulse.

He thought of Ana. He did not know her name. He did not know if she was in the next room, or if she had been beaten into silence, or if she had simply stopped existing, her hands replaced by a template.

He typed a word.

*hm,*

The red pin dropped. *Grammar error detected.*

He deleted it.

He looked at the wall. The light fixture rattled. *Thwack.*

He placed his fingers on the keys. He was going to answer the lighthouse. He did not know how to be Ana. He did not know how to be Grace. He only knew that the silence was too heavy, and the thudding would not stop if he did not type.

He began to type.
