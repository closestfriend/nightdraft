The dashboard glowed the color of a bruise. Lee did not look up from his tea when he slid the tablet across the laminate surface. The screen displayed a red banner, CONVERSION OVERDUE, beneath a graph that had not moved in eleven days.

"Grace Lin," Lee said. "Account 409-C. You have the handoff notes and the archive. Read every message. Do not type until the supervisor confirms you are aligned."

Sam nodded. The tablet screen was cracked near the bottom corner. He tapped it open. The chat log began in blue text, then darkened to grey as the months passed. He scrolled. The previous operator, Ana, had written a steady stream of short, polite replies. *Hello, how is your day? I am busy with the quarterly reports. I hope you are resting.*

In the next room, the wall vibrated. A sound like a damp towel being struck against a heavy board. *Thwack. Thwack.* It stopped. Then started again, faster. Sam’s knee moved in a small, involuntary sync. He forced it to still.

He read on. Paul Whitaker had sent an image of a lighthouse in fog. Ana had replied: *It is very beautiful. I hope you can see the light clearly.* Paul had sent a two-thousand-word essay on the sociology of coastal isolation, referencing Thoreau and a 19th-century maritime survey. He had asked, in a footnote, if Ana thought the keeper’s solitude was a form of labor or a form of penance.

Ana had replied: *It is very beautiful. 😊😊😊*

Sam stopped reading. He stared at the three emojis. They hung in the white space of the chat window, bright and useless. He had worked the line before, handling accounts that moved in cycles of hunger and shame. He knew the mechanics of the trap. He knew how to mirror a target’s vulnerability to create a sense of shared history. He knew the exact phrasing that made a stranger feel seen.

He had not felt anything in weeks. The work was a current; he just swam in it.

Sam opened the guidelines document for Grace Lin. *Persona: Corporate. Style: Professional, warm but restrained. Tone: Supportive. Do not engage in philosophical debate. Do not over-share. Conversion goal: Deposit to verify identity.*

He looked back at Paul’s essay. The man had written about the lighthouse keeper’s log, the way the wind eroded the stone, the specific geometry of the beam. He had written, *I think the keeper is not waiting for rescue. He is waiting for someone to notice the work itself.*

It was a good point. It was a sharp, lonely point.

Sam felt a twitch in his jaw. He looked at the beating. The rhythm had changed. It was slower now, a heavy, wet thud that rattled the light fixture. He did not look toward the door. He looked at Ana’s three emojis.

*Too emotionally reciprocal,* Lee had written in the review notes for Ana. *She is engaging with the target’s intellectual output rather than the deposit. She is failing to convert.*

Sam’s finger hovered over the keyboard. He could write a reply. He could say, *I am sorry, I was unavailable. Please send the verification code.* It was the correct thing. It was the thing that kept the lights on and the walls quiet.

But the feeling was there, small and sharp, like a splinter. Contempt. He looked at the three emojis again. Ana had not been bad at her job. She had just been blind. She had seen a man and thought he was a wallet. Paul had not been a wallet. He had been a man sitting in the dark, holding up a stone and asking if anyone could see the light it made.

Sam’s hands were cold. The wall thudded again. *Thwack.*

He did not type the standard response. He placed his fingers on the keys. The cursor blinked in the text box, a steady, impatient pulse. He was going to write something. He was going to answer the lighthouse. He did not know what that meant. He only knew that if he did not type the answer, the silence in the room would become too heavy, and the thudding would never stop.

He began to type.
