The guidelines sat pinned to the cubicle wall, laminated, the supervisor’s thumbprint visible in the lower corner where he’d pressed it flat after the last revision. Kyan read them twice, aloud, not because he needed to memorize commas and heart emojis, never semicolons, but because the procedure required vocalization to confirm comprehension, and the microphone near his station had been active since the morning handoff. “Voice: warm, curious, slightly scattered, never clinical, commas and heart emojis always, never semicolons, emotional reciprocity permitted only within conversion parameters, and QA will flag any deviation that suggests authentic feeling over strategic feeling.” He spoke the words into the air, feeling them settle like dust on the partition, and then he turned to the archive.

The archive was not a file but a river. Six months of messages, Aung’s voice, or what the compound had decided her voice was. Kyan scrolled. The first message from Mark, six months back, a single line: “Are you real?” Then Aung’s reply, the architecture of intimacy built in increments, the commas falling like beads, the heart emojis clustering where a semicolon might have lived in an honest sentence. Kyan looked for the quirk. It was not in the punctuation but in the breath, the way she paused before answering, the way she used “I think” not as doubt but as a door held open. “I think I want to say yes,” she wrote, and the comma after “think” was always there, never omitted, never replaced by a period. She used “maybe” three times in every long paragraph, a tic that turned suspicion into tenderness. She never used the word “lonely” directly; she used “waiting for something that doesn’t arrive,” and she paired it with a heart emoji that was never alone, always doubled, as if one were a mistake.

He found her typing style in the metadata, a ghost in the server logs. Aung typed with her left hand, the archive’s rhythm slightly slower, her sentences beginning with lowercase “i,” her commas slightly heavier, her heart emojis rendered in a particular shade of pink that the guidelines did not specify but the software preserved. Her emotional inflections were physical: she apologized for her English three times in the first week, then never again, as if the apology itself were a bridge that, once crossed, could not be rebuilt. She asked Mark about his childhood not as a question but as a confession, “Tell me if you were ever left alone in a room,” and he answered, and she answered back with a comma and a heart, and the archive grew.

Kyan copied the style into his draft window. He wrote: “i think, maybe, that i have been waiting for something that doesn’t arrive, and i’m not sure if that is loneliness or just the feeling of being recognized, which is different, which is something else, which is more than i should say, but here it is,” and he inserted the doubled heart emojis, and he felt the seam open in his chest, the translation software rendering his thoughts into her cadence, the software’s interface showing a green check beside the word “commas,” a yellow flag beside “emotional reciprocity,” and he knew he was performing not a person but a procedure.

The supervisor passed again, collarless, the shirt’s hem frayed where he’d bitten the thread. He did not stop at Kyan’s station. The dashboard on the left screen flashed: CONVERSION OVERDUE. The performance improvement plan was a document in the drawer, signed by Kyan’s own hand, mentioning the beatings in the next room that continued while the office kept working, mentioning that discouraging a larger deposit, or being too emotionally reciprocal, would earn a flag, and that a flag meant a score, and that a score meant punishment, and that the procedure was the punishment, and that the punishment was the procedure.

Kyan opened the handoff note from Aung, written in her dialect, the punctuation irregular, the commas scattered like teeth in a mouth that had been broken and mended. “He asks about books,” she wrote. “He asks about your childhood. He asks if you are real. Answer with commas, never semicolons, and do not answer too quickly, or he will think you are a procedure, which you are, which is the point, but he must not know it, which is also the point.” Kyan read it twice, feeling the brutality of the procedure, feeling how the comedy arrived with the horror, how the system described procedures minutely until they became emotionally revealing, how the archive was not a history but a weapon, how Aung’s absence was not a mystery but a procedure, how her punishment or reassignment was a detail in a larger mechanism that did not care about her.

He typed into the reply window, the translation software mediating between his thoughts and Mark’s language, the screen splitting into two panes: the left in English, the raw message from Mark about a book, about loneliness, about the feeling of being recognized, and the right in the account’s voice, the commas falling, the hearts doubling, the lowercase “i” holding his shame like a cup. He wrote: “i think, maybe, that i have been reading the same book you mentioned, and i feel like i am inside it, like i am the page that is turned and turned again, and i am not sure if that is love or just the idea of being understood, which is different, which is something else, which is more than i should say,” and he added the doubled heart emojis, and he watched the QA annotation appear, a green check beside “commas,” a yellow triangle beside “emotional reciprocity,” a red pulse beside “conversion overdue,” and he knew that he was clever, self-aware, and still wrong about himself, that his intelligence did not protect him from humiliation, that he could manipulate strangers but could not lie convincingly about himself, that the account was not a person but a corporate procedure, and that he was the procedure now.

The message went out through the translation layer, rendered in her voice, in Aung’s voice, in the voice of a woman who was not a woman, and Kyan sat back, the wall thinner than it looked, the supervisor’s collarless shirt visible in the screen’s reflection, the beatings continuing in the next room, the archive growing, the dashboard pulsing, the handoff note still open, and he did not turn away from the reply window where Mark’s next message was already loading, the cursor blinking in the account’s voice, the procedure holding him in place, the false intimacy real enough to hurt, and he knew he had to answer, that the answer was already written in the guidelines, that the guidelines were the only truth he had left, and that he was wrong about himself, and that he was wrong about her, and that the wrongness was the work, and that the work was the only thing that was real.
