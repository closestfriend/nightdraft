The shift started with a slap of rubber on concrete, then the supervisor’s collarless shirt. Kyan was assigned the romantic account, six months deep, and he was told to read the archive before he spoke as her. The archive was a wall of messages, and he read every one, scrolling through Aung’s handwriting in the handoff notes, the commas and heart emojis, the commas and heart emojis, the commas and heart emojis, never a semicolon, never a semicolon, never a semicolon, and he felt the seam in his jaw, the place where he could lie to strangers but never to himself.

The guidelines document was pinned to the wall beside the translation software. Commas and heart emojis, never semicolons. QA flags violations. He read it once, twice, until the procedure revealed him: he was a conscripted worker who could manipulate strangers and was shamed by it, and he was about to inherit a fabricated intimacy that Aung had built with an American who was almost impossible to scam for money but embarrassingly susceptible to intellectual intimacy. The account was named something in Burmese, a compound word for endurance, and Kyan knew that Aung had written its formative months, that the archive contained the architecture of a false self that he was now required to inhabit.

He opened the chat interface. The translation software glowed, mediating between operator and target, turning Mark’s English into the compound’s working tongue and back again. The archive loaded in batches of fifty messages, each one a small violence of affection. Aung had written in her dialect, the commas and heart emojis, never semicolons, and Kyan could feel the shape of her voice in the text: clever, self-aware, still wrong about herself. He read until his eyes burned, until he understood that Mark was intelligent, online, suspicious, lonely, and that he had been corresponding with her for six months, that the relationship was not a relationship but a procedure.

The dashboard rose beside him. A CONVERSION OVERDUE alert pulsed red. The supervisor had assigned him the account because Aung was gone, perhaps punished, perhaps moved, and Kyan was meant to convert the intimacy into a deposit. He was on a performance improvement plan already, the beatings in the next room while the office kept working, and he knew that discouraging a larger deposit, or being too emotionally reciprocal, would earn him a flag. The persona rules were texture, not quotation; he showed them at most once, in the way he checked the guidelines, in the way he refused the semicolon that lived in his own thoughts.

He found the last message from Aung, a handoff note in her dialect, describing the account’s vulnerabilities: Mark loved language, loved the idea of being understood, loved the intellectual intimacy that was not love but its simulation. Kyan read it twice, feeling the brutality of the procedure, the way the comedy arrived with the horror, the way the system described procedures minutely until they became emotionally revealing. He opened the reply window.

The translation software was already running. Mark’s last message was in English, a long paragraph about a book, about loneliness, about the feeling of being recognized. Kyan translated it, not into English but into the account’s voice, into her voice, into Aung’s voice, and he felt the seam open. He could manipulate strangers; he could not lie convincingly about himself. He typed a reply with commas and heart emojis, never semicolons, and he watched the QA annotation appear, a green check, and he knew that he was wrong about himself, that his intelligence did not protect him from humiliation, that the account was not a person but a corporate procedure, and that he was the procedure now.

The supervisor passed behind him, his collarless shirt visible in the reflection of the screen. Kyan did not turn. He kept reading, kept translating, kept becoming her, and the archive kept growing, and the dashboard kept alerting, and the beatings kept happening in the next room, and he wrote back, and the message went out, and the reader was pulled forward into the false intimacy that was not false at all, but real enough to hurt.
