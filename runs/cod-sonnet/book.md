# Account 4471

*When a trafficked worker in a border scam compound inherits a six-month romance account, the lonely American on the other end has already worked out that the photographs are stolen but stays for the mind behind them, and the two men keep the fiction going until a collapse forces a meeting in which the messages the American loved most turn out to have been written by someone else.*



## Chapter 1: Six Months of Someone Else

Lee gave him the account on a strip of receipt paper, the way he gave everything: a login, a password, and a persona name in block capitals. GRACE LIN. Under it, in the same ballpoint, *6 mo. — OVERDUE.* On Sam's dashboard the account sat third from the top in a red row, and the alert pulsed at the pace of a sleeping animal. CONVERSION OVERDUE. Someone had once decided the letters should breathe.

"You read first," Lee said. "All of it. System won't let you type until you do."

The system was thorough about this. The archive opened at message one, and each message was locked on screen for a dwell time proportional to its length before the scroll bar would advance. A short "good morning" held him nine seconds. He could not skim. Twice, out of habit, he tried to, and a small grey box informed him that engagement was insufficient and returned him to the top of the message.

Through the wall, someone was being hit. It had a rhythm he had learned to file under weather: the flat slap of a hand or a belt, a pause, and a voice counting in a language he only half had. Nobody at the long tables looked up. The girl beside him was mid-sentence to a man in Ohio, and her keys never broke stride. A supervisor walked the aisle with a thermos and adjusted the oscillating fan so it would stop blowing on the papers at the far end. Sam read that Grace Lin was fond of oolong.

She was fond of oolong, of her late grandmother's garden in Taichung, of "a quiet life with meaningful conversation." The guidelines document had put these in her mouth, and Ana, whose operator ID stamped the first four months, had kept every one of them in sight like a woman carrying eggs. She used ellipses. She said "haha" and "hmm" and "you are so sweet." She asked Paul how his day was, and Paul, who was fifty-one and worked in municipal water infrastructure outside Dayton, told her, at length, with footnotes.

That was the first thing. Paul footnoted.

Sam read through the third month, the fourth. Paul described a dispute with his neighbor over a property line, and Grace said *Oh no, that sounds so stressful 🥺.* Paul described the same dispute again, this time as a problem in the philosophy of boundaries, and Grace said *You think so deeply, I love this about you.* Paul went quiet for two days. Then, in the fifth month, at 1,974 words by the counter in the corner of the screen, Paul sent the essay.

It was about lighthouse keepers. It began with the Flannan Isles and moved to the automation of the lights in the 1970s, and then it made an argument, a real one, that the keeper had been the last job in which a man's loneliness was considered part of the equipment. Paul had cited a keeper's log. He had disagreed with a historian by name. In the last paragraph he asked whether anyone was ever paid to be watched for, and whether Grace thought that was different from being loved.

The dwell timer held Sam on that message for four minutes and ten seconds. He would have stayed longer.

Grace's reply was three red hearts.

He scrolled back up to be sure, and the grey box scolded him. He read the reply again for its mandatory seven seconds. Three hearts. Ana had put a full stop after them, as though they were a sentence.

Something in the next room gave a high sound, and the counting stopped, and started again from one.

She had not misjudged the money; the money was a separate failure, and Sam could see the ratio on the dashboard, the red row, the breathing. She had misjudged the man. Paul had not been reaching for a woman. He was reaching for an opponent. Every message that went unanswered on the merits had sent him back to his neighbor, his footnotes, his six months of patient testing for a hole in the floor. Ana had heard *lonely* and reached for the only instrument she had been issued, and the instrument was hearts.

It was a small, hot, private feeling, and it took him a moment to place it, because it had been weeks. It was contempt, and it belonged entirely to him. Nobody had assigned it. He held it the way he had once held a coin found in a coat.

The scroll bar reached the bottom. The grey box dissolved, and the reply field opened, white, with the cursor going on and off.

He typed *Paul,* and deleted it. He typed *The Flannan log,* and left it there, and behind the wall the counting reached nine.

The guidelines document was forty-one pages and lived in a tab that Sam had kept closed since his first week, the way one keeps a cupboard closed on a smell. He opened it now and let the cursor blink over the reply field while he read.

Section 3.2 was titled *Voice: Punctuation and Affect.* Grace used commas freely, ellipses "to indicate reflection or warmth (maximum two per message)," and hearts. Red hearts were for affection, yellow for gladness, and the white heart was reserved for "moments of shared grief, e.g. references to the late grandmother." No message might carry more than three hearts, unless the user had sent one first, in which case the allowance rose to four. Under 3.2.4 was one line, bolded: **Grace does not use semicolons.** A footnote explained that semicolons "read as educated, correcting, or male," and that QA would flag every instance. Sam had seen the flags. They came back on the operator's screen as small orange tabs at the margin, each with a code and a number, like a coat check.

He went looking for Ana in the orange tabs. In month two she had been flagged for a semicolon, one, in a paragraph about tea. The annotation read *Ops: reminder, see 3.2.4,* and Ana had not, as far as Sam could tell, ever been reminded again. She had simply learned to build sentences that never needed the thing. He admired the joinery. Then she had been flagged four times for *grey,* and the annotation there had worn thin, from *see 3.1, American spelling* to just *3.1* to just a small orange arrow. She had kept spelling it the wrong way. Whatever else was being taken from her, she had kept this one vowel.

After that he could see her everywhere, the way a person who has learned one word of a language starts hearing it on the street. She opened a turn of thought with *hm,* lowercase, a comma trailing it like a hand on a sleeve. *hm, I think you might be right about the pumps.* She did not do it when she was stalling; she did it when she was actually deciding. And her long letters, the ones that ran past nine hundred words, ended on a small separate line: *ok?* Never *okay,* never a question mark doubled. It asked for nothing, and Paul, Sam noticed, always answered it, sometimes only with *ok.*

The ferry letter was in month three. The counter said 1,212 words. It was Grace at nine years old on the boat out to Penghu, sick over the rail, her grandmother behind her holding the hem of her jacket so she would not go over, not scolding, just holding, and afterward the kitchen on the island where the old woman fried an egg in a pan the size of a saucer and cut it into eight so it would last. Sam read it slowly, as required, and then a second time, which nobody required. There was a sentence about the smell of diesel and ginger sharing a stairwell. There was no heart in the entire letter. At the bottom, on its own line: *ok?*

Paul's answer had come eleven hours later. *I printed this. It's pinned over my desk, next to the pressure-zone map. My neighbor asked what it was and I said it was a work document. That's the first time I've lied to him.* Then, below it, in the same message, the thing that Sam kept coming back to: *Whoever taught you to write like that should be paid more.*

The dashboard did not know what to do with this. It logged the reply as *Engagement: HIGH* and, next to it, in the small grey type reserved for the machine's uncertainties, *Sentiment: mixed.*

Sam scrolled forward through month four, watching for her. The *hm* thinned. A *grey* appeared in the second week of the month, and one more, and then a *gray* that looked to him like a sentence written by someone wearing gloves. He had credited the full stop after the three hearts to her, in the last scene of the archive, but there was no *hm* near it and no *ok?* and he could not now say why he had been so sure. After the fourth month there was nothing of her at all. The letters got shorter. Every comma sat in its issued place.

He typed her name into the archive's search field. It indexed message text only. It returned no results, and beneath the empty list it offered, helpfully, to search again with fewer words.

Behind the wall the counting had stopped altogether. Sam put his hands on the keys and wrote *hm,* and looked at it there, small and lowercase in the white field, with the comma held out.

Paul's message arrived with the dwell timer already running, and Sam was made to sit through it: ninety seconds of grey bar for a hundred and forty words, the send button dimmed so that he could not skim.

*Can I borrow you for a work thing? I'm translating a harbour-pilot manual. Volunteer project, no one will ever read it. One sentence has beaten me. "The pilot is released by the master when the vessel no longer requires him." Released sounds like a prisoner. Dismissed sounds like a maid. The original verb is closer to "let go with thanks," which English doesn't have. Grace, you're good at the thing English doesn't have.*

The guidelines had a section for requests of this kind. *Grace defers. Grace is delighted to be asked. Grace does not claim expertise she has not shown.* Sam had the section open in the other window. He had meant to be quick and correct, a paragraph of pleasant agreement, the ratio nudged.

He typed *hm,* and the rest came from somewhere the guidelines did not index.

*hm, my mother used to save the heels of the bread in a biscuit tin. Nobody in our house would eat them. She'd take the tin out to the back step every evening and crumble them for the birds, and if you asked why she said they weren't leftovers, they were just going on to someone else. Maybe that's the verb... "sent on." the pilot isn't thrown away, he's passed along to the next thing that needs him; it's a handing, not a firing. Does that help, ok?*

He sent it. Then he read it, because the system permitted reading after sending, and saw the semicolon halfway through, small and upright, exactly where a person with a good school behind him would put it. He had not felt his finger reach for it. The bread was true. The tin had been blue, with a Scottish castle on the lid. He had not given anyone the tin in eleven months, and he had just given it to a water engineer in Ohio, punctuated.

Paul's answer took under a minute. Sam watched the typing indicator stop, start, stop.

*Sent on. God. Yes. That's it, that's the verb. I'm going to lose a whole afternoon to the birds now.* Then, on its own line: *Who are you tonight?*

The dashboard did not wait for Sam to decide what that meant. A yellow tag dropped beside his message:

*QA-PUNCT-03: semicolon detected (educated / correcting / male). Operator: SAM. Account: G. LIN. Severity: standard.*

Lee came across the floor without hurrying. He carried a clipboard, though everything on it was also on a screen, and he stood behind Sam's chair for the length of time it took to read the tag twice. He smelled of the menthol sticks he kept behind his ear. "First one," he said.

"It's one mark."

"It's one mark today." Lee wrote on the clipboard. He wrote slowly, the way a man does when he wants the pen heard. "Ana had one. Month two. After that, never. You know what she did?"

"No."

"She deleted the key." He tapped the sheet. "Not really. But she told everybody she did." He tore nothing off and left nothing behind; the log was already updated, Sam could see the small red *1* appear on the corner of his own name. "Answer him. The clock is running on the man. And Sam, nothing about who you are tonight. You are who you were yesterday."

Behind the far wall a chair went over. Nobody at the desks looked up. The woman two seats down tapped her enter key twice, rhythmically, as if to cover the sound with a better one.

A small grey line had appeared under Paul's question, in the type the platform used for events: *Paul took a screenshot.*

Sam looked at that for as long as the timer would let him not be typing. Somewhere in a house outside Dayton there was now an image of his mother's bread, the blue tin, the semicolon, lying in a folder beside the pressure-zone map. The translation panel offered three suggestions for a reply to *Who are you tonight?* The first was *Just me… why?* with a pale-blue heart. Sam cleared it. He put his hands on the keys and wrote, in lowercase, *hm,*



## Chapter 2: Ratio

On the seventh morning, Lee put the ratio on the wall monitor, where it was visible to the whole floor and to the two men who came in to fix the air conditioner. Each operator was a row. Each row had a number and a small bar, and the bars were green up to a certain length and then were not. Sam's row read GRACE LIN / PAUL W. / DEPOSITS: 0 / RATIO: 0.00, and beside it the CONVERSION OVERDUE marker pulsed at a rate somebody had chosen.

"Zero is a clean number," Lee said. "Nobody can argue with it." He held out a form. It was one page, with a box for the operator's name and a box for the operator's signature, and between them a list of behaviours with checkboxes. Sam read the two that had been ticked: *Persona too emotionally reciprocal (sustained)* and *Insufficient urgency in pipeline advancement*. Under *Support provided*, someone had typed, *Variant B.*

"Variant B is what?"

"Vulnerability first." Lee said it as if he were reading the name off a tin. "Variant A asks. Variant B confides, and the man offers. Ana's Variant A. Yours is B. We see which is the better shape." He pointed with the pen. "You sign there."

Sam signed there. The pen was chained to the desk, and the chain was just long enough to sign with and not long enough to lean back.

The script arrived in the sidebar, marked *APPROVED / DO NOT ALTER TONE*. It was four paragraphs. Grace had been holding something in. Her uncle's shop in Taichung had closed and the loan was in her name. There was a figure in brackets, and a deadline in brackets, and a line that read *I've never told anyone this, I don't know why I'm telling you* followed by a broken-heart emoji, which the guidelines did not permit in any colour.

He cut the bracketed figure. He cut the deadline. He replaced the broken heart with the plain grey ellipsis Ana had used when she meant *I'll stop here*. He left the uncle, because the uncle was in the script, and because he could not think of a better uncle. When the dwell timer released the send button he sat with his finger over it for a while, and then he sent it.

Paul's answer took a little over an hour, and it was long enough that Sam's own dwell timer locked the reply field while he read. It was still counting down at the bottom of the screen after he had finished, so he read it a second time to have something to do.

*Grace, I'm sorry. That's a lousy thing to carry alone, and I'm glad you said it. I don't think you should hand anyone money before you've seen the whole picture, so here's what I'd do, and you can tell me if I'm wrong.*

*1. Write down every debt, the rate, the minimum. Just the list. Don't decide anything yet.*
*2. Call a nonprofit credit counsellor (link below; they're free for the first session, and the good ones are accredited). Do not use anyone who calls you first.*
*3. Ask about consolidating the loan into one payment. Ask whether the shop's closure changes the terms.*
*4. Send me the list if you want. I'll check the arithmetic. I like arithmetic. ok?*

Below it was the link, and below the link was a line by itself: *Also the thing about the uncle is not your fault, and I'd like you to write that down too.*

Sam read the last line with the timer still going. Then the field unlocked and there was nothing to do.

Lee had come up behind him with the clipboard. On the clipboard was a printout of the message, on paper, which meant that someone had walked it to the printer and back while Sam was reading. Lee took a red pen from behind the other ear and circled a word in the second numbered item, and the circle was closed and neat, a good one.

"Counsellor," Lee said.

"He's trying to help her."

"He is trying to help her get out." Lee drew a second circle inside the first, which was not necessary. In the next room something heavy was set down and then dragged, and the woman two seats over began to tap her enter key again. "In the ratio, what is this?"

Sam looked at the printout. "It's a link."

"It's a link." Lee held the page up beside the wall monitor as if the two could be compared. "So you tell me. Where in this does the man put his hand in his pocket?"

Ben's station went red a little after the meal break. It pulsed in the corner of the wall monitor beside his account name, and Lee read the name aloud, not loudly, the way a person reads a number off a ticket at a pharmacy.

Ben finished his sentence first. Sam watched him do it. He took the sunflower-seed cup from beside his keyboard, folded the top edge over so the shells would not spill, and set it in the bin. Then he removed his headset, wound the cord around two fingers, and hung it on the hook, because headsets were counted at the end of shift and a headset on a hook was one less thing to explain. Two men Sam had not seen before were standing at the door of the next room. They were polite about it. One held the door.

The chat on Ben's screen was still open. A customer named Dale, or someone using Dale, had written *are u there baby* and the response window had turned amber. A woman from the far row was sent over to sit in Ben's warm chair. She adjusted the height, read forty seconds of archive, and typed, *sorry, I dropped off, my phone died*. Sam recognized the lie because he had used it himself on Tuesday.

Then the sound started. It was not loud. It was a wall, and the sound was what a wall does with it: a flat, patient, regular sound with pauses in it where somebody was getting his breath or somebody was resetting. Nobody looked up. The woman two seats over typed faster, and then the room found it, all thirty keyboards, until the typing was a rain on a roof and the roof had a drum under it. Sam realized he was typing *and honestly, I think* in the same time. He stopped. His hands hovered over the keys with the cursor blinking after *think*. Then, because a hovering hand shows on the activity graph, he finished the sentence with something about tea.

When it was over, Ben was walked back to his seat. His chair was occupied, so the woman gave it up and he sat in it. He put the headset back on. He did not have the cup, so he did nothing with his hands.

Lee updated the wall monitor. Under Ben's name a yellow band appeared and the word PIP. A moment later, under Sam's, in the same yellow, the same word, and after it a column Sam had never noticed had a header: REVIEW. A date was in it. It was a Friday, eleven days away. Somebody had known that date before this morning. It had been sitting in the system beside his name the whole time, like the fee in a contract, and the only thing that had changed was that it was now displayed.

So he wrote to Paul at length.

Paul had said, two weeks back, that a water main was a record of every decision anyone had made about a town, and Sam took that and built on it for six hundred words. He wrote that a pipe replaced section by section was the same pipe only in the ledger, and that maybe a person was that too, a ledger with a continuous name. He disagreed with Paul about lead service lines, cautiously, in a way that gave Paul room to be right. Ana would have been proud of the *hm,*. He put it in twice. The message's dwell timer, when Paul's answer came, would be long, which meant Paul would sit with it, which meant the account would be alive at the end of the week. Sam did not phrase it to himself that way. He told himself he was writing well.

Near the end, Paul had mentioned that his father had run the same line of work and had never once discussed it at dinner. Sam wrote: *I think I've been afraid of that too, of being the kind of person who is only ever at work.*

The flag arrived before the send confirmation.

`QA / ACCT: GRACE_LIN / OP: SAM / SEV 2`
`TAG: EMOTIONALLY RECIPROCAL. Persona discloses fear in response to target disclosure.`
`Guideline 4.3: Grace receives. Grace does not match. Recommend Variant A. Cumulative: 2.`

Lee had not even turned his head. Sam retrieved the message from the queue. He deleted the sentence one letter at a time from the end, because select-and-delete left a mark in the log, and when it was gone the paragraph closed over the place where it had been without a seam. The message was better without it, and he could see that it was. He put *ok?* at the bottom, lower case, one question mark, the way she had.

Paul did it at the kitchen table with the laptop on a dish towel, because the laptop ran hot and the table had been his mother's. He right-clicked the photograph of Grace at the teahouse, the one with the paper lantern and the cup held in both hands, and chose *Search image with*. He had done this to a hundred faces at work, contractors' badge photos and the utility board's new hire. He had never done it to a face that had told him about her grandmother's loquat tree.

Forty-one results, sorted by similarity. The first was a stock-photo site that had taken the image from somewhere else. The fourth was a Portuguese-language page for a dental clinic in Curitiba. Under the lantern and the cup, in a carousel captioned *Nossa equipe*, stood a hygienist named Camila Reis, who had, according to her bio, a special interest in gum health for children. She wore the same cardigan. Someone had cropped out the clinic's waiting room to leave a teahouse behind her.

Paul opened a spreadsheet. He logged the URL, the date of the earliest crawl (fourteen months before he had ever heard of Grace), and the photograph's filename, and he ran the other eleven photographs through the same procedure, one at a time, waiting each time for the little wheel. Nine were Camila. He had been a man who read a leak survey down to the last valve number, and he found that he could not stop at nine. He screenshotted the results page, then the screenshot folder, then the spreadsheet, in case somebody should ask him someday to prove that he had been careful.

He wrote *Who are you* into the reply box and looked at it. He deleted it letter by letter. He wrote *I know about Camila* and deleted that. What he sent, after the third attempt, was: *What can you see from where you're sitting? I mean literally.*

The message was eleven words. Its dwell timer on Sam's side ran four seconds, short enough that the system did not seem to care about it, and Sam read it three times inside them.

He knew what it was. Nobody asked a person what she could see from her chair unless the chair was in doubt. Grace's chair was, according to her file, a wicker one on a balcony in Taipei with a view of Elephant Mountain. Ana had written that balcony in month two, with a potted calamansi.

Sam looked up. The wall in front of him was the colour of old teeth. There was a fan on a bracket that ticked once per revolution, where a blade had a chip. Beyond the window, which was painted shut and blinded with a sheet of cardboard, there was a river. He could hear it at night when the keyboards stopped, and he had never seen it.

He was a poor liar about himself. He had known it since the first day, when Lee had asked him his name for the roster and he had given it. He typed:

*hm, ok. a wall, mostly. the colour of old teeth... a fan that ticks every time it turns. and a river i can hear but not see. i think it's a river. it sounds like one. that's all i've got, ok?*

The translation software rendered it into Paul's English, more or less as written, and the QA panel did not flag it, because there was nothing in the message a rule was written for. Sam pressed send. He had left out the balcony. He had left out Taipei. He sat with his hands flat on the desk and heard the fan tick.

In Dayton, Paul read it once at the dwell rate and once again without it. He took a screenshot. Then he typed, *Thank you. That's a real answer. What else?* and sent it before he could put anything in front of it.

On the other side, the new message arrived with its timer already running down. Sam looked at the wall, then at the window with the cardboard on it, and began to think about the floor.



## Chapter 3: The Return

Ana came back on a day the fan was ticking faster than usual, and Sam did not recognise her from behind. He recognised the chair. It was Ben's old one, which had been given to no one since, and she pulled it out from the desk beside his as though she had only stepped away for water. She had been a month in the other building. She was thinner in the wrists and the neck, and she held her left hand in her lap, below the desk edge, the way people hold something they have been told not to show.

Lee stood behind them with a printed sheet. He liked to announce arrangements on paper.

"Grace is now two shifts," he said. "Sam has days. Ana has nights. The target must experience one woman with a regular sleep pattern and one mind. If he detects a change of hand, the fault is in the handoff, not in the person. Handoff is a required field."

He set the sheet between their keyboards. It was a template. Under *Mood at close* and *Open threads* there was a line reading *Seam Risk (0–5)*, and beside it, in Lee's block capitals, *ANSWER 0 OR 1. A 2 IS AN ADMISSION.*

Then he went to the front and turned the ratio board toward the room, and the room got on with its work.

Ana read the archive first, all of it, starting at the top, as the rules required. Sam had never seen anyone take the dwell timers so calmly. She sat through the ones that ran six minutes and did not touch the mouse. When she reached the ferry letter she stopped scrolling and sat with her good hand flat beside the keyboard. Sam waited, and did not know what he was waiting for.

"You gave her a father at a railing," Ana said. She did not look at him. "With a thermos. In the good coat."

"It was a ferry. You said, in the guidelines, she likes the water."

"She likes tea. Her father is in the file as gone at six. There's the garden and there's the grandmother and there's a mother who counts things. I wrote that. I put a gap where he'd be, because a gap is safe. You filled it with a man and a thermos." She scrolled up two screens and back. "'The coat smelled of diesel and oranges.' She's never smelled that coat. I know where you smelled it. I don't need to be told."

"Paul answered," Sam said. "Eleven hours. He said it was—"

"I read what he said." She said it with no anger, as one reads out a temperature. "He wrote back because it was true. That's the trouble with it. It's true, and it belongs to nobody on the account."

The fan ticked. At the next station a man was reciting a wire transfer procedure to himself.

"And the semicolon," Ana said. "In the second week I got flagged for one. Lee read it out. I don't use them anymore. I don't miss them." She pulled the handoff template toward her and, in the *Seam Risk* box, typed a 1, then deleted it and typed a 0. "It's a gap. He doesn't need to see the gap. He needs to see her being certain."

She took a pen from her sleeve. Sam had not known she had one; pens were counted. On the back of the template, in a hand that was small and level, she began to write, and he read it upside down.

*1. hm, is how she thinks. not how she talks. use it once a day, ok?*
*2. grey. always. if the software fixes it, let it, but type it.*
*3. she ends long ones with ok? one question mark.*
*4. no father. if he asks, she changes the subject to tea.*
*5. anything she remembers, check the archive first. if it isn't there, she doesn't.*

"That last one is the whole job," Ana said. "You'll want to break it. Everybody does. Something true will come up and it'll fit so well."

Sam turned the page toward himself. Her handwriting slanted slightly backward, as if leaning away from the words. He took his own pen from where he had hidden it and copied the list onto the inside of his wrist, small, then blotted it against his sleeve when it began to smear.

"Which of these did you break?" he asked.

Ana opened the handoff field and began her first note for the night, and he saw her type the words *Mood at close: steady.*

"Four," she said. "Once."

He built it in the draft pane while Ana read the night queue beside him, and the hard part was not the idea. The software stood between him and Paul, and it rearranged whatever he gave it. A sentence that left him beginning with *Home* came out the far side beginning with *In the house of*. He ran the first line through four times, swapping verbs, until the English at the left edge held still on an H.

*Home is a word I have been turning over all week, the way you turn a cup to find the side without the chip.*

*Every kettle my grandmother owned was dented, and she would not let anyone replace them.*

*Leaves open slowly if you let them. I have never been able to teach that to anyone I love.*

*Please tell me about the pipes, the real ones, the ones under Dayton that nobody thanks. Where do they run? Who walks them? I want to picture where you stand when you say you work, ok?*

He checked the left edge. H, E, L, P. He checked it again in the outbound preview, where the software had put a gray box around each paragraph and helpfully offered to merge them into one. He declined the merge.

Ana's eyes had moved from her queue to his screen. He didn't see them move. He saw the cursor of her attention stop, the way a fan ticks differently when something is in its blades. She read the draft the way she had read the archive, top to bottom, lips not moving. She reached the fourth paragraph and stayed there for as long as it takes to read four lines twice.

She did not look at him. She reached across and, with one finger, deleted a comma after *pipes* that Grace would not have used, and put it back where she wanted it, after *real*. Then she pulled the handoff template toward her and wrote, under *Pending outbound:* *W. Paul, tea/home/work. Approved, A.*

"Grey," she said, to the screen. "You didn't use it. Doesn't matter. It's not in this one."

He sent it.

Paul answered in under two hours, which was fast for Paul, and the message was long. Sam read it in the preview pane before it had finished loading, top to bottom, as he had learned to.

*Grace,*

*I have to tell you something embarrassing, which is that I read your letter three times before I saw it, and the third time only because I was reading it out loud to the dog. Down the left side: H, E, L, P. Very good. I assume you did that on purpose as a test of me, or else I've caught you in the act of the thing I'm about to describe.*

*It's called apophenia. Klaus Conrad, 1958, describing early schizophrenia, though we all do it and the mild version is just called being a person. We are pattern engines with no off switch. Faces in outlets, Jesus in toast, the Bible code people who found the assassination of Rabin in Genesis by skipping every 4,772nd letter. Somebody ran the same method on Moby-Dick and got the death of Princess Diana. Give a mind a grid of letters and enough patience and it will hand you whatever it's afraid of.*

*So I did the arithmetic. Four lines, four specific letters. Initial-letter frequencies in English being what they are, H, E, L and P are all fairly common openers, and you write in short paragraphs, which multiplies the chances. I went back through your last six messages and found PIPE, TEA, and, I swear, DAM. I'm not saying you're not clever. I'm saying that the thing that makes it feel like a message is the thing in me that wants one.*

## Chapter 4: The Long Night

Paul wrote to Camila Reis the way he wrote to residents about a boil-water advisory: what he knew, how he knew it, what he did not know, whom to contact. It took four drafts. She answered before he had finished lunch. Her English was quick and had no punctuation to spare. She had known for two years. Eleven men so far had written to her with a version of his message, each one kind, each one wanting her to see that *he* was the one who'd noticed. Did he want a medal?
