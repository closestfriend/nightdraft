The office had a machine that gave out numbers, and Paul had taken one, and then, seeing Sam at the far table with a caseworker's folder in front of him, had put it back in the slot, where it did not go. The machine printed a second one. Paul carried both across the room.

"You're supposed to wait for 114," Sam said.

"I'm 117. I'm claiming a rounding error."

The table was bolted to the floor. The pen was bolted to the table by a beaded chain about the length of a forearm, so that anyone who wished to write had to sit at the angle of a person being arrested. Sam had already found the angle. He looked older in the face and exactly as he had sounded, which Paul had not prepared for, having spent six months building a voice a person could not have.

Paul unpacked his folder. He had printed everything at the library in Dayton, the librarian assuming he was a man in a legal dispute, and had stapled each item twice, top left, because the staple was where he kept his nerves. He laid down the first. The header read *Grace Lin*, then a date from the second month.

"The ferry letter," he said.

Sam did not touch it. He read the first paragraph upside down. "That isn't mine."

"It says she's crossing to the island with her grandmother's rice jar on her knees, and she's afraid to put it down because the water might take it and the jar's the only thing she owns that has a lid." Paul tapped the page. "*Realy*, one L. Ellipses, always three, never four. I thought it was a tic of hers. I built a whole personality on that spelling."

"It was a tic of Ana's."

"Who's Ana."

"Who wrote that." Sam turned the page a quarter turn with one finger, the way he might have turned a plate. "And the first four months. The garden in Taichung. She'd been there once, on a school trip, maybe. I don't know. She wrote the childhood letter. She wrote that Grace hated coriander."

Paul took out the second printout and slid it beside the first. This one had a blue highlighter line under a sentence, and in the margin, in his own hand: *SEMICOLON?* He had circled it twice.

"Then this is you."

"Yes."

"*I keep a room in myself that I don't let the work into; it has a window, and I am afraid of what I'll see out of it if the work finds the door.*" Paul read it flat, as a man reads a pressure log. "I checked that against everything she'd sent before. Nothing. Then I checked the ferry letter, and the gap between them was so wide I decided one of them was lying. I decided it was the second."

"It was the second."

"It was the *first*," Paul said, and heard himself, and stopped. The caseworker behind the glass looked up and looked down. Paul lowered his voice to the register of a man reporting a leak. "I argued Kierkegaard with a committee. I thought I'd found one person who could hold up her end. I never met her. There wasn't a her to meet. There was a shift change."

"There were two people who both meant it," Sam said. "That's not the same as a committee."

"Is that supposed to help?"

"No." Sam looked at the chain. "It's just the accurate version. I'm not good at the other kind."

Paul laughed once, badly, through his nose. Then he turned the ferry letter over. The back was blank, the printer's white, a thing nobody had ever addressed.

"Put it down," he said. "Whatever she's called. The one who wrote the rice jar. I don't want to keep calling her a typo."

Sam took the pen on its chain and bent to the angle, and the beads ran out across the table and pulled taut. He wrote in capitals first, as the forms wanted, and looked at them, and thought they looked like a person being booked. Under them he wrote it again in lowercase, slowly, with the accent she'd once told him nobody in that building was ever going to put on it.

Paul read it upside down, then right side up, and said it aloud with the stress on the wrong syllable.

"Second one," Sam said. "Again."
