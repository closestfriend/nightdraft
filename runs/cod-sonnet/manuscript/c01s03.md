Paul's message arrived with the dwell timer already running, and Sam was made to sit through it: ninety seconds of grey bar for a hundred and forty words, the send button dimmed so that he could not skim.

*Can I borrow you for a work thing? I'm translating a harbour-pilot manual. Volunteer project, no one will ever read it. One sentence has beaten me. "The pilot is released by the master when the vessel no longer requires him." Released sounds like a prisoner. Dismissed sounds like a maid. The original verb is closer to "let go with thanks," which English doesn't have. Grace, you're good at the thing English doesn't have.*

The guidelines had a section for requests of this kind. *Grace defers. Grace is delighted to be asked. Grace does not claim expertise she has not shown.* Sam had the section open in the other window. He had meant to be quick and correct, a paragraph of pleasant agreement, the ratio nudged.

He typed *hm,* and the rest came from somewhere the guidelines did not index.

*hm, my mother used to save the heels of the bread in a biscuit tin. Nobody in our house would eat them. She'd take the tin out to the back step every evening and crumble them for the birds, and if you asked why she said they weren't leftovers, they were just going on to someone else. Maybe that's the verb... "sent on." the pilot isn't thrown away, he's passed along to the next thing that needs him; it's a handing, not a firing. Does that help, ok?*

He sent it. Then he read it, because the system permitted reading after sending, and saw the semicolon halfway through, small and upright, exactly where a person with a good school behind him would put it. He had not felt his finger reach for it. The bread was true. The tin had been blue, with a Scottish castle on the lid. He had not given anyone the tin in eleven months, and he had just given it to a water engineer in Ohio, punctuated.

Paul's answer took under a minute. Sam watched the typing indicator stop, start, stop.

*Sent on. God. Yes. That's it, that's the verb. I'm going to lose a whole afternoon to the birds now.* Then, on its own line: *Who are you tonight?*

The dashboard did not wait for Sam to decide what that meant. A yellow tag dropped beside his message:

*QA-PUNCT-03: semicolon detected (educated / correcting / male). Operator: SAM. Account: G. LIN. Severity: standard.*

Lee came across the floor without hurrying. He carried a clipboard, though everything on it was also on a screen, and he stood behind Sam's chair for the length of time it took to read the tag twice. He smelled of the menthol sticks he kept behind his ear. "First one," he said.

"It's one mark."

"It's one mark today." Lee wrote on the clipboard. He wrote slowly, the way a man does when he wants the pen heard. "Ana had one. Month two. After that, never. You know what she did?"

"No."

"She deleted the key." He tapped the sheet. "Not really. But she told everybody she did." He tore nothing off and left nothing behind; the log was already updated, Sam could see the small red *1* appear on the corner of his own name. "Answer him. The clock is running on the man. And Sam, nothing about who you are tonight. You are who you were yesterday."

Behind the far wall a chair went over. Nobody at the desks looked up. The woman two seats down tapped her enter key twice, rhythmically, as if to cover the sound with a better one.

A small grey line had appeared under Paul's question, in the type the platform used for events: *Paul took a screenshot.*

Sam looked at that for as long as the timer would let him not be typing. Somewhere in a house outside Dayton there was now an image of his mother's bread, the blue tin, the semicolon, lying in a folder beside the pressure-zone map. The translation panel offered three suggestions for a reply to *Who are you tonight?* The first was *Just me… why?* with a pale-blue heart. Sam cleared it. He put his hands on the keys and wrote, in lowercase, *hm,*
