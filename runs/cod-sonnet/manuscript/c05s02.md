The fans stopped before the lights did. Sam had never heard the room without them, and for a moment the silence had a shape, forty chairs' worth of people holding still. Then the screens went, row by row, and the emergency strip over the door came on the color of weak tea.

Ana's hand stayed on the keys. She typed *the color of* and nothing appeared, because nothing was there to receive it.

Down the corridor a door opened that was never opened, and a man's voice came through a loudspeaker in a language Sam had heard only through walls. It said the same sentence twice. The second time it was followed by a sound like furniture being taken apart.

"That's not ours," said Ana.

Her laptop had a battery. It showed the dashboard for the length of a breath: the account, the number, and across the top, in the red they kept for it, CONVERSION OVERDUE. It had said *pending review* an hour ago. Sam understood that the screen had simply gone back to the last thing it was certain of. Then the internal channel gave one line, from Lee, and the battery took the rest.

*.*

They stood there looking at it, and both of them read it as they always had, as an instruction.

Lee went past the glass door with the cash box against his stomach like a child, the tape still on his wrist, a strip of it stuck to his cuff where he had forgotten to tear it. Two of the floor supervisors ran behind him carrying nothing, which meant their pockets were full. Nobody looked into the room. Someone in the corridor was calling a name, and someone else was saying *not yet, not yet*, to a locked door.

Ana went the other way, into Lee's office. Sam followed as far as the frame. She knelt at the tower under the desk, which he had not known was a tower, and pressed the side panel with her thumb until it gave. Inside was a drive the size of a bar of soap, with a strip of Lee's tape across it and *GL* in Lee's square capitals. She pulled it, and the cable came with it, and she coiled the cable around two fingers and tore it free.

"Six months," she said. "Mine are in there. Yours. All of Paul." She wiped the drive on her jumper as if it were wet. "Hm. Give me your hand."

He gave it. She put the drive in his palm and closed his fingers over it, and to do that she had to bring her left hand out of the sleeve. He looked at it. She let him look for as long as it took to close the last finger, and then it went back in.

"There's a girl on four," Ana said. "Mai. They lock the rooms on four from the outside, and none of those men are going to remember that."

"I'll come."

"You'll go down the back stairs with that, and you'll go over the wall by the generator, where the fence is cut. You know where. Everyone knows where, it's how the cigarettes come in." She was already turning. "Don't wait at the bottom."

"Ana."

"Put it somewhere they'd have to undress you to find." Then she was in the corridor, and the emergency strip gave her back to him for three doors, a grey jumper going upstairs against everyone else.

He did it in the stairwell, on the landing with the broken pane. He took the two squares out of his trouser pocket, the ones left from the day's eight, unfolded them, and laid the drive in them like something being served. Folded once, and then twice, exact. He put the parcel inside his sock and walked on it down the rest of the stairs, and a corner of it found the bone of his ankle at every step.

The yard was full of engines and torchlight. He went along the wall, past the generator, which was ticking as it cooled, and through the gap, and there was the river, wider than it had ever looked from the fourth-floor window and the color of nothing he had a word for yet. A man stood in a long flat boat at the mud edge and held out one hand to each person, and took nothing, and asked nothing, and pushed off when it was full. Sam sat on the wet plank between a woman with no shoes and a boy holding a rice cooker.

On the far bank he climbed out and turned around. The building was dark except for one window on the fourth floor where a light moved. Below it, people were coming through the gap in the fence in ones and twos. Sam stood with his hand on his ankle and counted them as they came down to the water, looking for grey.
