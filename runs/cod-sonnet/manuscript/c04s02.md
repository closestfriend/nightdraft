At the start of it the dashboard was already red. CONVERSION OVERDUE sat in the corner of Sam's screen in a box that cycled every ninety seconds, going bright, then dim, then bright, so that the ticking of it was in his peripheral vision like a bad tooth. Under it Lee's messages stacked in the internal channel. *Status?* Then *Status.* Then, eleven minutes later, a single full stop, which Sam understood to be the most severe thing Lee had ever sent.

Paul had come back. It was afternoon in Ohio, and Paul had asked whether Kierkegaard's leap of faith had to leap toward anything, or whether the leaping was the whole content of it. Sam had read the question four times. He had a summary, one page, which he had memorised in the second week and never expected to use. He typed his answer in his own language and the software rendered it in a preview pane, a yellow bar underneath reading CONFIDENCE 71%.

He defended the knight of faith. He wrote that the knight does not leap in the dark, that the knight is the one who trusts he will be caught. The software turned this into something gentler than what he had meant. Then, because a quotation would give it weight, he added one, from memory, from the summary: *As Kierkegaard says, "Faith is the certainty that one will be caught."* He checked the pane. 71%. He sent it.

Paul's reply came fast, and it was long, and it began with a *ha*.

*I'm sorry, I laughed out loud in my kitchen, which I needed. He never says that. The whole point is that Abraham is NOT certain. He believes "by virtue of the absurd." You've turned Kierkegaard into a trust fall. The line you may be reaching for is that the knight of faith looks like a tax collector. Nobody could pick him out of a crowd. That's the part that gets me. Where did you get "caught"?*

Sam put his fingers on the keys and held them there. Lee's full stop was still at the bottom of the channel. He began, in his own language, *In the Taichung house there was*, and stopped, and deleted it, and the deletion made a small sound in the room, because the room was that quiet.

The door opened at 4:10. Sam knew it was 4:10 because the dashboard printed the missed-quota timestamp on the sheet one guard held, and the guard read it aloud, as procedure required, before either of them took an arm. They took him to the next room. It was the room with the drain, and it was smaller than the sound of it.

Ana slid into his chair before it had stopped turning. She put her left hand below the desk, where she kept it, and with the right she scrolled up through Paul's message once, quickly, the way a person checks a pot. She lowered the seat two notches. She wrote:

*hm. If it's a leap, someone has to be on the other side. I'll be here, in whatever way here means.*

She read it over. She did not add *ok?*, since it wasn't long enough for that. She sent it.

Paul's answer took under a minute.

*That's the one.*

When Sam came back there was blood in his collar and a wet, warm thread of it running from the corner of his mouth to his jaw, and he was holding his sleeve against his nose. The room had been rearranged around his absence. Ana stood beside the chair with her arms folded, the left one tucked in. The screen showed the thread. He read it from the top, standing, his sleeve going dark: his trust fall, Paul's *ha*, the tax collector, and then her two sentences, small and level, with a comma exactly where a comma was needed. The box in the corner had stopped cycling. It was green, and it said CONVERSION: PENDING REVIEW.

A drop from his chin landed on the T key. He wiped it with his cuff, which only spread it.

"You sit," Ana said, and stepped back from the chair. It was still set two notches too low. He sat down into it, with his knees high, and did not reach for the lever.
