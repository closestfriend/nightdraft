Ana came back on a day the fan was ticking faster than usual, and Sam did not recognise her from behind. He recognised the chair. It was Ben's old one, which had been given to no one since, and she pulled it out from the desk beside his as though she had only stepped away for water. She had been a month in the other building. She was thinner in the wrists and the neck, and she held her left hand in her lap, below the desk edge, the way people hold something they have been told not to show.

Lee stood behind them with a printed sheet. He liked to announce arrangements on paper.

"Grace is now two shifts," he said. "Sam has days. Ana has nights. The target must experience one woman with a regular sleep pattern and one mind. If he detects a change of hand, the fault is in the handoff, not in the person. Handoff is a required field."

He set the sheet between their keyboards. It was a template. Under *Mood at close* and *Open threads* there was a line reading *Seam Risk (0–5)*, and beside it, in Lee's block capitals, *ANSWER 0 OR 1. A 2 IS AN ADMISSION.*

Then he went to the front and turned the ratio board toward the room, and the room got on with its work.

Ana read the archive first, all of it, starting at the top, as the rules required. Sam had never seen anyone take the dwell timers so calmly. She sat through the ones that ran six minutes and did not touch the mouse. When she reached the ferry letter she stopped scrolling and sat with her good hand flat beside the keyboard. Sam waited, and did not know what he was waiting for.

"You gave her a father at a railing," Ana said. She did not look at him. "With a thermos. In the good coat."

"It was a ferry. You said, in the guidelines, she likes the water."

"She likes tea. Her father is in the file as gone at six. There's the garden and there's the grandmother and there's a mother who counts things. I wrote that. I put a gap where he'd be, because a gap is safe. You filled it with a man and a thermos." She scrolled up two screens and back. "'The coat smelled of diesel and oranges.' She's never smelled that coat. I know where you smelled it. I don't need to be told."

"Paul answered," Sam said. "Eleven hours. He said it was—"

"I read what he said." She said it with no anger, as one reads out a temperature. "He wrote back because it was true. That's the trouble with it. It's true, and it belongs to nobody on the account."

The fan ticked. At the next station a man was reciting a wire transfer procedure to himself.

"And the semicolon," Ana said. "In the second week I got flagged for one. Lee read it out. I don't use them anymore. I don't miss them." She pulled the handoff template toward her and, in the *Seam Risk* box, typed a 1, then deleted it and typed a 0. "It's a gap. He doesn't need to see the gap. He needs to see her being certain."

She took a pen from her sleeve. Sam had not known she had one; pens were counted. On the back of the template, in a hand that was small and level, she began to write, and he read it upside down.

*1. hm, is how she thinks. not how she talks. use it once a day, ok?*
*2. grey. always. if the software fixes it, let it, but type it.*
*3. she ends long ones with ok? one question mark.*
*4. no father. if he asks, she changes the subject to tea.*
*5. anything she remembers, check the archive first. if it isn't there, she doesn't.*

"That last one is the whole job," Ana said. "You'll want to break it. Everybody does. Something true will come up and it'll fit so well."

Sam turned the page toward himself. Her handwriting slanted slightly backward, as if leaning away from the words. He took his own pen from where he had hidden it and copied the list onto the inside of his wrist, small, then blotted it against his sleeve when it began to smear.

"Which of these did you break?" he asked.

Ana opened the handoff field and began her first note for the night, and he saw her type the words *Mood at close: steady.*

"Four," she said. "Once."
