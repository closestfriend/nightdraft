The guidelines document was forty-one pages and lived in a tab that Sam had kept closed since his first week, the way one keeps a cupboard closed on a smell. He opened it now and let the cursor blink over the reply field while he read.

Section 3.2 was titled *Voice: Punctuation and Affect.* Grace used commas freely, ellipses "to indicate reflection or warmth (maximum two per message)," and hearts. Red hearts were for affection, yellow for gladness, and the white heart was reserved for "moments of shared grief, e.g. references to the late grandmother." No message might carry more than three hearts, unless the user had sent one first, in which case the allowance rose to four. Under 3.2.4 was one line, bolded: **Grace does not use semicolons.** A footnote explained that semicolons "read as educated, correcting, or male," and that QA would flag every instance. Sam had seen the flags. They came back on the operator's screen as small orange tabs at the margin, each with a code and a number, like a coat check.

He went looking for Ana in the orange tabs. In month two she had been flagged for a semicolon, one, in a paragraph about tea. The annotation read *Ops: reminder, see 3.2.4,* and Ana had not, as far as Sam could tell, ever been reminded again. She had simply learned to build sentences that never needed the thing. He admired the joinery. Then she had been flagged four times for *grey,* and the annotation there had worn thin, from *see 3.1, American spelling* to just *3.1* to just a small orange arrow. She had kept spelling it the wrong way. Whatever else was being taken from her, she had kept this one vowel.

After that he could see her everywhere, the way a person who has learned one word of a language starts hearing it on the street. She opened a turn of thought with *hm,* lowercase, a comma trailing it like a hand on a sleeve. *hm, I think you might be right about the pumps.* She did not do it when she was stalling; she did it when she was actually deciding. And her long letters, the ones that ran past nine hundred words, ended on a small separate line: *ok?* Never *okay,* never a question mark doubled. It asked for nothing, and Paul, Sam noticed, always answered it, sometimes only with *ok.*

The ferry letter was in month three. The counter said 1,212 words. It was Grace at nine years old on the boat out to Penghu, sick over the rail, her grandmother behind her holding the hem of her jacket so she would not go over, not scolding, just holding, and afterward the kitchen on the island where the old woman fried an egg in a pan the size of a saucer and cut it into eight so it would last. Sam read it slowly, as required, and then a second time, which nobody required. There was a sentence about the smell of diesel and ginger sharing a stairwell. There was no heart in the entire letter. At the bottom, on its own line: *ok?*

Paul's answer had come eleven hours later. *I printed this. It's pinned over my desk, next to the pressure-zone map. My neighbor asked what it was and I said it was a work document. That's the first time I've lied to him.* Then, below it, in the same message, the thing that Sam kept coming back to: *Whoever taught you to write like that should be paid more.*

The dashboard did not know what to do with this. It logged the reply as *Engagement: HIGH* and, next to it, in the small grey type reserved for the machine's uncertainties, *Sentiment: mixed.*

Sam scrolled forward through month four, watching for her. The *hm* thinned. A *grey* appeared in the second week of the month, and one more, and then a *gray* that looked to him like a sentence written by someone wearing gloves. He had credited the full stop after the three hearts to her, in the last scene of the archive, but there was no *hm* near it and no *ok?* and he could not now say why he had been so sure. After the fourth month there was nothing of her at all. The letters got shorter. Every comma sat in its issued place.

He typed her name into the archive's search field. It indexed message text only. It returned no results, and beneath the empty list it offered, helpfully, to search again with fewer words.

Behind the wall the counting had stopped altogether. Sam put his hands on the keys and wrote *hm,* and looked at it there, small and lowercase in the white field, with the comma held out.
