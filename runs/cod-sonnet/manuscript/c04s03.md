The dormitory had eleven bunks and a sheet taped inside the door that listed who slept in which. Sam's had been crossed out and rewritten so many times that the paper had gone soft. He lay on it with two twists of toilet paper in his nostrils. The ration was eight squares a day, and he had counted what he'd used and would have to account for it.

Ana sat on the bunk across from him with her back against the frame and her feet drawn up. Her left hand stayed inside the sleeve of her jumper. Neither of them had spoken since the corridor.

"You want to know why I took the chair," she said.

"You wrote it well."

"That isn't why." She looked at the ceiling, where a pipe ran the length of the room, sweating. "You'd stopped typing. There's a sound the room makes when a person stops. And he was sitting there in Ohio, and the little dots were going. On, off. On, off. I couldn't stand to hear him wait, that's all. It wasn't kindness. I'd have done it for a kettle."

Sam moved the tissue in his left nostril. It had stiffened. "The ferry letter," he said.

"Hm." She was quiet long enough that a man on the far bunk turned over. "My gran's kitchen. It's got nothing to do with Taichung. It's got a Formica table, and there's a radio that only got the shipping forecast, and she used to cut my toast into soldiers till I was fourteen. The ferry's from a holiday to Mull. I moved the sea about and changed the tea." She drew her sleeve down over her knuckles. "Six months he's been reading it. I wonder if he can taste the Formica."

She waited. It was a very ordinary wait, the kind you leave for someone to go next. Sam thought of his mother's hands, the way she peeled an orange in one long strip and set the strip on the windowsill to dry. He thought of the windowsill. He held the thought, felt its edges, and put it back where it had been, and said nothing. Ana watched him say nothing and did not push. That was in the rules too, he supposed, the handoff sheet's unwritten margin.

The lights flickered once, which was the ten-minute warning. Then the door opened without a knock and Lee stood in it with a clipboard and a sheaf of printed pages, one of which he peeled off and pressed to the door with tape from his wrist, where he kept a roll like a bracelet. He did not look at Sam's collar.

"Whitaker, P.," Lee read. "Has purchased a ticket. Friday. Five days." He let that sit the length of a breath. "Dayton to the meeting point, one way. Purchase confirmed by his screenshot of the receipt, which he sent to Grace without being asked. The account will be harvested on his arrival. Everything he has told her goes into the file. Everything he has, we take an inventory." He smoothed the tape with his thumb. "Operators on the account will not change tone before then. Any variance is reported to me. Ana, nights. Sam, nights. Both."

He tore off the next page and did not tape it anywhere. The door shut. The tape held, one corner already lifting.

They both looked at the sheet without going to read it.

"It can't end at the meeting point," Sam said. His voice came out thick from the tissue. "He'll be standing there."

"With a coat, probably. He'll have thought about the coat."

"There isn't a Grace to arrive."

"No." Ana pulled her sleeve back over the hand. "So it can't be her that says it. Whatever tells him not to go, if it's her, he'll argue. He argues with everything. It's how you got him. It's how I got him, in the end, if I'm honest, it wasn't the tea."

"Then it isn't her."

"It can't be me either. I'm too good at her. He'd hear the ellipses and stay." She said it flatly, an inventory. "You write it. Nothing from the sheet. No Taichung, no tea, no leaning on a comma. Plain words, the sort you'd say across a table."

"Lee reads everything before it goes."

"Lee reads what's in the box." She turned her head toward the pipe, the drip working down its seam. "Plain enough, it might look like a variant. Or like you've run out of variants. Either way it'll look like a man having a bad night."

The lights went out at the second warning. In the dark Sam heard her slide down the bunk and the springs take her weight.

"What's the first word?" she said.

He lay with the paper in his nose, running through the words he had, and found that all of them had already been used on someone.
