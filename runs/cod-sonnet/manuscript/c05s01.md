He typed *Paul.* and stopped, because the name sat there looking like the opening of a Grace message. Grace would have followed it with an ellipsis. He hit the period key and left the period.

*This is not Grace. I can't tell you who I am, and I'm not going to try, because I'm bad at lying about myself and you would hear it.*

*I am in a room with eleven other people. The wall in front of me is green, the flat green of a cheap hospital, and it is painted to the height of my shoulder. Above that it is bare concrete with pencil marks on it, like measurements. There is a pipe that drips. Every person here has eight squares of toilet paper a day and counts them. I have used six.*

*Don't come Friday. Please don't get on the plane. No one will be there who wrote to you.*

He read it once. He deleted nothing, added no comma he did not need, and pressed send with the side of his thumb, as if that were a smaller act than the finger.

The dashboard took eleven seconds. The tag arrived in the internal channel without a category, only a single full stop from Lee.

He did not run. He sat and watched Ana at the next station keep her eyes on her own screen and her left hand under the desk. When Lee came he brought no tape and no page. He read the message over Sam's shoulder, all of it, with his lips closed, and at *pencil marks* he reached past Sam and scrolled up to check the wall.

"Green," Lee said.

"Yes."

"It is not green. It is sage." He straightened. "Smaller room. Then your paper goes to two."

"Two a day?"

"Until I say." Lee wrote it on the back of his own wrist with a pen, small, and the numbers looked like a sum. "It is not a punishment. It is a reduction of variance."

The smaller room had a stool and no window. Sam sat for what he took to be the whole of a shift and did the arithmetic that was left to him. Two squares. Folded once, a square lasted. Folded twice, a person could be exact. He had not been hit, which he noticed and did not know what to do with.

When they brought him back the chair was Ana's again and the tissue in his nose was gone, so he bled a little on the way and held his sleeve to it. She did not get up. She turned the screen toward him with two fingers.

Paul had answered in nine minutes. The message was long and had no jokes in it, which was how Sam knew.

*I read it four times. I'm going to say what I did so you know it's true and not a thing I'm telling you. I copied the whole log, six months, every message, your letter about the ferry, the one with the semicolon, this one. I added what you gave me: the green wall, the pipe, the pencil marks, the eight squares. I sent it to a reporter in Columbus who wrote about a man in Akron last year, and I filled in the federal tip line, all the boxes, including the box that says "additional details," which I have never before had a use for.*

*I checked my own reasoning, because that's what I do. You may be a very good version of the thing I'm afraid of. But nobody optimizing for my money tells me not to buy the ticket. That is the one instruction that costs them.*

*I'm not coming Friday. I'm telling you so you can tell whoever needs telling. I'm at the kitchen table. I've put the coat back in the closet.*

*Write to me again if you can. Tell me the color of anything else.*

Sam read to the end and then, because the number was there and he had earned it, looked at the top of the screen where the account still said *Grace Lin, active.*

"Lee's going to read this," he said.

"Lee reads what's in the box," said Ana. Her right hand was already on the keys. "What's the color of anything else?"
