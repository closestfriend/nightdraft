He built it in the draft pane while Ana read the night queue beside him, and the hard part was not the idea. The software stood between him and Paul, and it rearranged whatever he gave it. A sentence that left him beginning with *Home* came out the far side beginning with *In the house of*. He ran the first line through four times, swapping verbs, until the English at the left edge held still on an H.

*Home is a word I have been turning over all week, the way you turn a cup to find the side without the chip.*

*Every kettle my grandmother owned was dented, and she would not let anyone replace them.*

*Leaves open slowly if you let them. I have never been able to teach that to anyone I love.*

*Please tell me about the pipes, the real ones, the ones under Dayton that nobody thanks. Where do they run? Who walks them? I want to picture where you stand when you say you work, ok?*

He checked the left edge. H, E, L, P. He checked it again in the outbound preview, where the software had put a gray box around each paragraph and helpfully offered to merge them into one. He declined the merge.

Ana's eyes had moved from her queue to his screen. He didn't see them move. He saw the cursor of her attention stop, the way a fan ticks differently when something is in its blades. She read the draft the way she had read the archive, top to bottom, lips not moving. She reached the fourth paragraph and stayed there for as long as it takes to read four lines twice.

She did not look at him. She reached across and, with one finger, deleted a comma after *pipes* that Grace would not have used, and put it back where she wanted it, after *real*. Then she pulled the handoff template toward her and wrote, under *Pending outbound:* *W. Paul, tea/home/work. Approved, A.*

"Grey," she said, to the screen. "You didn't use it. Doesn't matter. It's not in this one."

He sent it.

Paul answered in under two hours, which was fast for Paul, and the message was long. Sam read it in the preview pane before it had finished loading, top to bottom, as he had learned to.

*Grace,*

*I have to tell you something embarrassing, which is that I read your letter three times before I saw it, and the third time only because I was reading it out loud to the dog. Down the left side: H, E, L, P. Very good. I assume you did that on purpose as a test of me, or else I've caught you in the act of the thing I'm about to describe.*

*It's called apophenia. Klaus Conrad, 1958, describing early schizophrenia, though we all do it and the mild version is just called being a person. We are pattern engines with no off switch. Faces in outlets, Jesus in toast, the Bible code people who found the assassination of Rabin in Genesis by skipping every 4,772nd letter. Somebody ran the same method on Moby-Dick and got the death of Princess Diana. Give a mind a grid of letters and enough patience and it will hand you whatever it's afraid of.*

*So I did the arithmetic. Four lines, four specific letters. Initial-letter frequencies in English being what they are, H, E, L and P are all fairly common openers, and you write in short paragraphs, which multiplies the chances. I went back through your last six messages and found PIPE, TEA, and, I swear, DAM. I'm not saying you're not clever. I'm saying that the thing that makes it feel like a message is the thing in me that wants one.*

*I'll take it as a compliment that you'd hide something in a letter for me, even by accident. The best puzzles are the ones nobody admits to setting.*

*The pipes, since you asked: cast iron, mostly, some from before the war. I'll tell you about the one under Third Street.*

*P.*

Ana had stopped reading her own screen. She was looking at the back of the handoff sheet, at the five rules in her small, level hand.

"He's good," she said.

Sam pulled his sleeve down over his wrist. Under the cuff the ink had run into a blue bruise where rule four had been.

"He counted it," he said. "He did the odds."

"Yes."

"Then what does he—" He stopped, because the dashboard had raised a small green tab in the corner, ENGAGEMENT: HIGH, and beside it the box where he was meant to type a reply. "What do I say to *dam*?"

Ana uncapped the pen again. "You say hm," she said. "You've got one for today."
