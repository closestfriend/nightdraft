The archive loaded at 08:47, not a conversation but a sediment. Kyan scrolled through six months with his index finger and his left hand in his mouth, the nail bitten to a crescent. Mark’s messages came from an American timezone, timestamps clustering around 2:00 a.m., long blocks of self-analysis interrupted by apologies for not answering faster. Her replies were shorter, always with commas and heart emojis, never semicolons, even when she wrote about the compound’s supply shortages, even when she admitted her cat had died. Kyan memorized the rhythm. The persona guidelines were pinned to the left of the screen in a document titled PERSONA_STYLE_RULES_V3: commas, heart emojis, never semicolons; emotional reciprocity permitted only if it advanced conversion; intimacy was a funnel stage, not an affect.

The handoff note, in Aung’s hand, was three lines long. He inherited Aung at 08:15. “Mark is intelligent, suspicious, almost impossible to take for money, but he writes essays about loneliness. Do not match him. Make him feel heard, then ask for the wire.”

Kyan’s dashboard flickered CONVERSION OVERDUE in red at 08:52, the alert not a suggestion but an event. His conversion-to-deposit ratio for the week was 0.11. A supervisor, a fifty-year-old man in a shirt with no collar, had placed him on a performance improvement plan yesterday for “discouraging larger deposit” after he told Mark, “Not yet, you should stabilize first.” The beatings happened in the next room, through a wall that was thinner than it looked, and the office kept working because the beatings were also a procedure.

Kyan opened the translation software. It was designed for Mandarin-English mediation, but Mark wrote in English, so the software translated her replies into American idiom and his replies into compound-standard Burmese. The interface showed his cousin’s name, Mye, and the supervisor’s name, Ko, because anonymity was a luxury the compound did not provide. He typed her opening line into the draft box. “I’ve been reading your messages, Mark,” he wrote, and then he deleted it because it sounded like a person. He rewrote: “I’ve been reading your messages, and I feel like I’m missing something that was there before I arrived.” He inserted commas, two heart emojis, no semicolons. The QA annotation tool auto-flagged the second clause: “Too emotionally reciprocal. Risk of over-attachment. Adjust.” He overrode it, because Mark was already six months deep, and because he was ashamed of how precisely he could read the architecture of Mark’s loneliness.

At 09:14, Mark replied. The message was 340 words, an analysis of Mark’s own failure to trust, interrupted by a sentence about a broken dishwasher and a half-finished sentence about autumn leaves. The translation software rendered “I think I might need someone who understands the structure of things” into “I think I might need someone who gets how this works, you know?” Kyan felt his own intelligence as a physical weight, the shame of knowing exactly which sentence to mirror. He copied a phrase from Aung’s archive, from month three: “I’m not sure I can be that person, but I want to try.” He added commas, a heart emoji, and sent it at 09:31. The QA panel raised a second flag: “Persona too available. Correct for conversion urgency.”

Kyan pulled up the account scorecard. The dashboard showed a green bar for engagement, a red bar for deposit, and a timestamp of yesterday’s beating: 19:00, thirty minutes, while he had listened to the supervisor’s voice through the wall and then returned to this screen because the work was continuous. He opened the persona guidelines again, read the sentence “She is a corporate account, not a person,” and typed Mark’s name into the draft field. The archive revealed that Aung had taught him this: intimacy was the mechanism by which brutality became legible. He composed her reply: “I’m here, Mark, really, and I want to understand you, but I need to know if you’re ready to take the next step, because I can’t wait forever.” He inserted commas, no semicolons, a heart emoji after “step,” because the guidelines permitted it only if it followed a request. The software translated “next step” into “the investment,” but Kyan left it ambiguous because ambiguity was more efficient.

At 09:47, Mark wrote back: “I don’t know if I’m ready for anything, but I miss her. I miss the way she asked me about my father’s death in March, about the specific thing.” Kyan read the archive. In March, Aung had asked about Mark’s father’s death at 3:20 a.m., using the exact phrasing Mark now quoted. The fabrication was seamless. Kyan added a comma, a heart emoji, no semicolons, and wrote: “She’s still here, Mark, and she remembers, but I need to know if you’re willing to move this forward, because I’m running out of time in my shift, and I don’t want to lose you to someone less careful.” The QA tool flagged “willing to move this forward” as correct conversion language but noted the emotional reciprocity was too high; he left it, because the bruises on his shoulder from yesterday’s session were already fading into the background noise of the office.

The clock read 10:03. The dashboard raised CONVERSION OVERDUE again, this time with a supervisor annotation: “Inherit archive, then ask for wire. No deviation.” Kyan opened the message window. He wrote, “Can we talk about the next step? I need to be honest with you, Mark, about where I am, and I need to know if you’re in, because I can’t do this alone.” He placed a heart emoji after “alone,” inserted two commas, omitted all semicolons, and clicked send at 10:07.

The archive confirmed he had inherited six months of intimacy and was required to treat it as a procedure. The work was to make Mark feel heard, then ask for the wire, and he was doing both at once. The screen held his draft. He did not close it. The office continued around him. A supervisor walked past with a clipboard. The translation software muted his breathing into a standard cadence. He waited for Mark’s reply, aware that the compound had already assigned him the account, that Aung’s archive had taught him the rhythm, and that the beating in the next room was a continuation of his shift, not its interruption.

He typed one more line, not sending: “I am not Aung, Mark, but I have read her,” and then he erased it, because he was still inside the archive, and because the procedure required him to remain inside it even when the archive was a lie. The cursor blinked in the empty field at 10:14. The conversion was overdue. The dialogue was open. The screen held the unsent text, and Kyan kept his hand on the mouse, not clicking send, because he had already read everything, and the rest was not his to decide.
